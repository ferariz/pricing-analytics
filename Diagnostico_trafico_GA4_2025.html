¿Podemos medir el efecto de los cambios de precio con lo que tenemos?

Evaluación del método antes/después ajustado año-contra-año (YoY) para los 8 experimentos de pricing. En qué se puede confiar, en qué no, y qué haría falta. Foto 11-ago-2026.
Contexto
En julio-2026 cambiamos precios en producción en 8 destinos (Canadá −11%, Marruecos +15%, Perú −10%, México −12%, USA −5,4%, Italia −10,4%, España −10,1%, Suiza −4,3%). Un doc previo ya confirmó que esos precios nuevos aterrizaron correctos en el checkout. Este evalúa el paso siguiente: medir su efecto en los KPIs. El método es antes/después con ajuste YoY — comparamos la ventana post-lanzamiento contra la pre, y restamos el mismo movimiento del año anterior (2025) para descontar estacionalidad. Para las métricas que dependen de tráfico (Sessions/day, CR%, Revenue/Session), ese año-anterior sale de GA4. Acá miramos si ese baseline aguanta.
El baseline de tráfico de 2025 (GA4) no queda comparable — no usarlo para el ajuste YoY de las métricas de tráfico.

Hubo crecimiento real en 2025 (las orders subieron), pero las views de GA4 subieron 2–16× más, y el CR implícito (orders/views) cae dentro del año. Sea por instrumentación creciente, tráfico de baja intención u otro cambio de medición — la causa exacta es inferencia — el efecto observable es el mismo: la serie de views de 2025 no es un baseline comparable, y es justo el denominador de esas tres métricas.

1 · El challenge — ¿y si el crecimiento de 2025 fuera demanda real, no un problema de datos?
Objeción razonable: al mirar el tráfico de 2025 sube fuerte de junio a agosto. ¿No será estacionalidad real de verano, y entonces el baseline sí sirve? Para contestarlo miramos las orders, que vienen de BigQuery (checkout, fuente estable, sin la instrumentación de GA4): son el testigo limpio de la demanda.

El fondo: para medir el efecto de un precio hace falta un control concurrente — un A/B test.

Todo lo anterior comparte una misma raíz: no hay grupo de control. Comparar contra el pasado (YoY) arrastra estacionalidad, tendencia y el ruido de medición de GA4, y con un solo año previo no se puede validar. Un A/B con holdout aleatorizado —precio viejo vs nuevo al mismo tiempo, misma estacionalidad, misma instrumentación— elimina el contrafactual de raíz. Y de paso rescata las métricas de tráfico: como los dos brazos se miden igual y en simultáneo, la tendencia de cobertura de GA4 se cancela entre brazos. Lo que hoy es ruido irreparable, ahí se neutraliza.

Can we measure the effect of the price changes with what we have?

Assessment of the before/after, year-over-year (YoY) adjusted method for the 8 pricing experiments. What we can trust, what we can't, and what it would take. Snapshot 11-Aug-2026.
Context
In July-2026 we changed prices in production across 8 destinations (Canada −11%, Morocco +15%, Peru −10%, Mexico −12%, USA −5.4%, Italy −10.4%, Spain −10.1%, Switzerland −4.3%). A previous doc already confirmed those new prices landed correctly in checkout. This one evaluates the next step: measuring their effect on the KPIs. The method is before/after with a YoY adjustment — we compare the post-launch window against the pre window, and subtract the same movement from the prior year (2025) to net out seasonality. For the traffic-dependent metrics (Sessions/day, CR%, Revenue/Session), that prior year comes from GA4. Here we check whether that baseline holds.
The 2025 traffic baseline (GA4) isn't comparable — don't use it for the YoY adjustment of the traffic metrics.

There was real growth in 2025 (orders went up), but GA4 views grew 2–16× more, and the implied CR (orders/views) falls within the year. Whether from ramping instrumentation, low-intent traffic, or another measurement change — the exact cause is inference — the observable effect is the same: the 2025 views series isn't a comparable baseline, and it's precisely the denominator of those three metrics.

1 · The challenge — what if 2025's growth were real demand, not a data problem?
Fair objection: looking at 2025 traffic, it rises sharply from June to August. Isn't that just real summer seasonality — so the baseline is fine? To answer it we look at orders, which come from BigQuery (checkout, a stable source, without GA4's instrumentation): they're the clean witness of demand.

It's the best available estimate, but still directional, not reliable. Only CAN, PER and MAR come out conclusive; the rest don't, due to short post windows (17–26 days). And there's a deeper limit: DiD assumes 2025 is a good counterfactual for what would have happened in 2026 without the price change. With a single prior year, different seasonality per country, and short windows, that parallel-trends assumption can't be verified.
5 · Recommendation
  • Ship now, as directional: YoY/Adj for Orders, AOV and Net revenue (BQ) + this elasticity, flagged by window length, sign, and the counterfactual caveat.
  • Mark n/a: Sessions/day, CR% and Revenue/Session in their YoY adjustment, with this diagnostic attached.
  • For causal conclusions: the problem isn't fixed with more of the same kind of data — see below.
The bottom line: to measure a price effect you need a concurrent control — an A/B test.

Everything above shares one root: there's no control group. Comparing against the past (YoY) drags in seasonality, trend, and GA4's measurement noise, and with a single prior year it can't be validated. An A/B with a randomized holdout —old vs new price at the same time, same seasonality, same instrumentation— removes the counterfactual at the root. And it also rescues the traffic metrics: since both arms are measured the same way, simultaneously, GA4's coverage trend cancels across arms. What's irreparable noise today is neutralized there.
